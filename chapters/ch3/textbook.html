Chapter 3 · Textbook

Bonds

A bond is a loan. This chapter covers how a bond pays interest, why its price moves opposite to interest rates and yields, who issues bonds, how credit ratings work, and the risks a bondholder takes.

Section 3.1Bond basics

What a bond is

A bond is a debt security. When you buy one, you lend money to the issuer, and you become a creditor, not an owner. The issuer promises to pay you interest and to give the borrowed money back on a set date.

  • Par value (face value): the amount the issuer repays at maturity. On the exam, assume $1,000 par unless a question says otherwise.
  • Coupon rate: the yearly interest rate, as a percent of par. It is fixed when the bond is issued.
  • Maturity date: the day the issuer repays par and the bond ends.
  • Indenture: the legal contract that sets out all of the bond's terms. A trustee watches over it for the bondholders.

Because a bond is a loan, the interest is a legal obligation. If the issuer misses a payment it is in default, and bondholders can go to court. That is very different from a stock dividend, which the board can simply skip.

Bondholders also do not vote and do not share in the company's growth. In exchange, they are paid ahead of every stockholder if the company fails.

Interest and current yield

Interest is always figured from par, never from the market price:

Annual interest = coupon rate × par

A 5% corporate bond with $1,000 par pays 5% × $1,000 = $50 a year. Corporate bonds pay semiannually, so that is two payments of $25, six months apart.

Bond prices are quoted as a percent of par, and one point is 1% of par, which is $10. A quote of 95 means 95% of $1,000 = $950. The price moves up and down in the market, but the interest stays at $50 a year.

What you earn depends on what you paid. That return is the current yield:

Current yield = annual interest ÷ market price

Section 3.2Price and yield

Premium, par and discount

A bond can trade for more or less than par. Price and yield move in opposite directions (an inverse relationship):

Market priceCalledCurrent yield vs coupon rate
Below parDiscountYield is higher than the coupon rate
At parParYield equals the coupon rate
Above parPremiumYield is lower than the coupon rate

The coupon rate is sometimes called the nominal yield, because it is the "named" rate printed on the bond. It never changes.

Interest rates drive the price

An existing bond pays a fixed coupon, so its price has to adjust when market interest rates change:

  • Rates rise → new bonds pay more, so old bonds with lower coupons are less attractive → their prices fall, to a discount.
  • Rates fall → old bonds with higher coupons look better → their prices rise, to a premium.

Not every bond reacts the same amount. A bond's price is more sensitive to rate changes when it has:

  • a longer maturity (the fixed rate is locked in for longer), and
  • a lower coupon (less of the return arrives early).

Exam tip

Rates up, bond prices down. Rates down, bond prices up. Long maturity and low coupon mean the biggest price swings.

Yield to maturity and yield to call

Current yield ignores one thing: a bond bought at a discount also gains as it moves up to par by maturity, and a premium bond loses value on the way down. Yield to maturity (YTM) is the total annual return if you hold the bond to maturity. It counts the interest and the gain or loss against par.

For a bond at a discount, yield to maturity is higher than current yield, which is higher than the coupon rate. At a premium the order reverses. At par, all three are equal.

Bond trades atRanking of yields (highest → lowest)
DiscountYTM > current yield > coupon rate
ParYTM = current yield = coupon rate
PremiumCoupon rate > current yield > YTM

Yield to call (YTC) is the same idea, but assumes the issuer calls the bond at the first call date and pays the call price. Issuers tend to call when rates have fallen, which is when bonds trade at a premium, so for a premium bond the yield to call is the lowest yield and the one that matters most. The lowest of the possible yields is sometimes called the yield to worst.

Trading below / above par.

Section 3.3Issuers and ratings

Who issues bonds

Three big groups borrow in the bond market. Each gets its own chapter later, so this is only a first look.

IssuerWhat backs itTax treatment of the interest
CorporateThe company's ability to pay; can be secured by assets or unsecured (debentures)Taxed by federal, state and local governments
U.S. TreasuryThe full faith and credit of the U.S. governmentFederal income tax only; exempt from state and local income taxes
MunicipalA state or local government's taxing power or a project's revenueGenerally exempt from federal income tax

Treasury securities are grouped by how long they last: bills mature in a year or less and are sold at a discount, notes mature in 2 to 10 years, and bonds mature in more than 10 years. They are seen as having the lowest credit risk, so they pay the lowest yields.

Agency bonds come from government-sponsored organizations such as Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac and Ginnie Mae. Municipal bonds pay lower rates than similar taxable bonds, because the tax exemption is worth something to the investor.

Credit ratings

Rating agencies grade how likely an issuer is to pay. The three main agencies are Moody's, Standard & Poor's (S&P) and Fitch. A higher rating means lower risk and a lower yield.

CategoryS&P and FitchMoody's
Investment gradeAAA, AA, A, BBBAaa, Aa, A, Baa
High yield (speculative, "junk")BB, B, CCC and lowerBa, B, Caa and lower

The dividing line sits between BBB and BB (Baa and Ba at Moody's). Bonds at BB or below must pay a higher yield to attract buyers, because default is more likely.

Ratings can change. If an issuer is downgraded, its bond prices usually fall, and if it is upgraded, they usually rise.

Exam tip

Lowest investment grade: BBB (S&P) or Baa (Moody's). Anything below that is high yield.

Section 3.4Risks and suitability

Risks

  • Interest rate risk: when rates rise, bond prices fall. Someone who has to sell before maturity may take a loss. Long-term, low-coupon bonds are hit hardest.
  • Credit (default) risk: the issuer may fail to pay interest or principal. Lower-rated bonds carry more of it.
  • Inflation (purchasing power) risk: fixed interest and the returned par buy less as prices rise. High inflation also tends to push rates up and bond prices down.
  • Call risk: callable bonds are called when rates are low, so the investor loses the higher coupon.
  • Reinvestment risk: when rates fall, coupons and called or matured principal can only be reinvested at lower rates.
Exam tip

Treasury bonds have almost no credit risk but still have interest rate and inflation risk. Every fixed-rate bond has interest rate risk.

If the company fails, creditors are paid before any stockholder. The order of payment is:

Exam tip · Priority in liquidation

Unpaid wages → unpaid taxes → secured creditors → unsecured creditors → junior (subordinated) unsecured creditors → preferred stockholders → common stockholders. Bondholders are among the creditors.

Bonds suit investors who want regular income and safety of principal more than growth, and who can hold to maturity or understand that prices move with rates. Treasuries and high-rated bonds suit the most cautious investors. Investors seeking growth usually choose stocks.
