Chapter 1 · Textbook

Common Stock

What owning a share gives you, how shares are counted and valued, what shareholders can vote on, how dividends and splits work, how stock trades, who it suits, and how to read a company's financial statements.

Section 1.1Basic characteristics

Common stock is equity: a share is a slice of ownership in a corporation. A bondholder lends the company money and is owed interest and principal. A common stockholder owns part of the business and is owed nothing. You share in growth through a rising share price and any dividends the board chooses to pay.

That ownership comes with the most risk in the capital structure. Common stockholders have a residual claim: if the company is liquidated, they are paid only after everyone else. In exchange, their upside is unlimited, while creditors can never receive more than they are owed.

Share prices move on supply and demand. When more investors want to buy than sell, the price rises; when sellers outnumber buyers, it falls. Company results and investor mood drive both sides.

Two ways to make money

  • Capital appreciation: sell the shares for more than you paid. Buy at $20 and sell at $35, and you gain $15 a share.
  • Cash dividends: the company shares part of its profits with owners, usually every quarter.

Where profits go depends on the company. A growth company reinvests its earnings in new products, expansion and research, so it pays little or no dividend; investors are betting on a rising share price. A mature company has fewer big projects to fund, so it pays much of its excess profit out as dividends.

Exam tip

Most common stocks pay no dividend at all. Growth = reinvest; mature = pay out.

Cyclical = things people can skip (cars, restaurants, hotels). Defensive = things people always need (utilities, healthcare, food).

Section 1.2Rights of common stockholders

Owning common stock gives you seven basic rights:

  1. Receive dividends, pro-rata, when the board declares them
  2. Vote for the board of directors and on major corporate changes
  3. Inspect the company's books and records
  4. Keep your percentage of ownership (preemptive rights)
  5. Vote on stock splits
  6. Claim what is left of the assets if the company dissolves
  7. Transfer (sell or give away) your shares

Dividends

A dividend is a distribution of profits that the board of directors declares. Nothing requires a company to pay one, and a company can cut or suspend it at any time. Shareholders do not vote on dividends.

Dividends are paid pro-rata, in proportion to the shares you own. If the board declares $1.00 a share on 1,000,000 shares and you own 100,000 of them (10%), you receive $100,000, which is 10% of the payout.

Cash

The most common type, usually paid quarterly. Taxed as income in the year received; qualified dividends get lower capital-gains rates.

Stock

Additional shares instead of cash. Total value is unchanged. Not taxed when received; the cost basis per share goes down, and tax is due when the shares are sold.

Property

Something other than cash or the company's own stock, such as the company's products or shares of a subsidiary it spins off. Rare.

    Exam tip · Open orders on the ex-date

    Because the price drops by the dividend on the ex-date, open orders below the market (buy limits and sell stops) are reduced by the dividend amount unless marked "Do Not Reduce" (DNR). Orders above the market are not adjusted.

    Voting

    Common stockholders vote on electing the board of directors, stock splits, and major corporate changes such as mergers, acquisitions, and charter amendments (including authorizing more shares). They do not vote on dividends; the board decides whether to pay one and how much.

    Think of it like a government: shareholders elect the board, the board hires and fires the CEO and other senior executives, and those executives run the company day to day. The board sets the company's direction, executive pay and major policies.

    A shareholder who cannot attend the annual meeting can vote by proxy, a form (by mail or online) that authorizes someone else to cast their votes.

    Some companies issue more than one class, such as Class A and Class B, that differ in voting power. Founders often hold a class with 10 votes per share while the public class has 1 or none. Both classes still own the same company.

    Every share gets one vote per board seat up for election. How those votes can be spread depends on the method:

    • Statutory voting: you may cast no more than your share count for any single seat. 100 shares and 3 seats means at most 100 votes per candidate.
    • Cumulative voting: you get shares × seats votes in total and can split them however you like, including all on one candidate. 100 shares and 3 seats means up to 300 votes for one candidate.
    Try it · Statutory vs. cumulative voting

    How many votes can you give one director?

    Statutory: shares per seat

    Cumulative: shares × seats, any mix

    Exam tip · Who benefits

    Total votes are the same under both methods. Cumulative voting lets a holder stack votes on one candidate, which benefits minority (smaller) shareholders by giving them a better chance to elect at least one director.

    Books and records

    Shareholders have the right to receive audited annual reports and to see the shareholder list and meeting minutes. The right does not extend to detailed internal records or trade secrets. Public companies file their financial reports with the SEC, so any owner can read them.

    K = annual + audited. Q = quarterly + unaudited.

    Preemptive rights and dilution

    When a company sells new shares, each existing owner's slice gets smaller. That is dilution. Suppose you own 50,000 of 500,000 shares (10%) and the company issues 500,000 new shares. If you buy none, you now own 50,000 of 1,000,000 shares: 5%.

    Where the charter grants them, preemptive rights let existing holders buy new shares before the public, in proportion to what they already own. In the example, buying 50,000 of the new shares keeps you at 100,000 of 1,000,000: still 10%.

    Without an offering of rights, dilutive moves such as issuing new shares, convertible securities, warrants or employee stock options need shareholder approval.

    Common stockholders also have limited liability: the most you can lose is what you invested. If a company goes bankrupt owing $500 million, its creditors cannot come after your house or savings. Your maximum loss is what you paid; your maximum gain is unlimited.

    Rights and warrants

    Both give the holder the right to buy the company's stock at a set price. They differ in who gets them, how long they last, and whether that price starts below or above the market.

    Exam tip · Where warrants show up

    Many space companies that went public through SPAC mergers around 2021 had public warrants trading next to the common stock, because SPAC units usually bundle a share with a fraction of a warrant. Those warrants were exercisable at $11.50, above the $10 unit price, which is the "above market at issuance" rule in action. Exercising warrants creates new shares, so it dilutes existing holders.

    Exam tip · Telling them apart

    Short and cheap = right. Long and pricey = warrant.

    Stock splits and stock dividends

    A split changes the number of shares and the price per share in opposite directions. The total value of your position does not change, and neither does your percentage ownership. A forward split (such as 2-for-1) lowers the price to make shares more affordable. A reverse split (such as 1-for-10) raises the price, often to meet an exchange's minimum listing price. Par value is adjusted the same way as the market price, which is why shareholders vote on splits.

    A stock dividend works the same way, with a factor of 1 + the dividend percentage. A 10% stock dividend on 100 shares at $55 leaves you with 110 shares at $50: still $5,500.

    Exam tip · The formula

    New shares = old shares × (new ÷ old). New price = old price × (old ÷ new). Check your answer: shares × price must equal the same total before and after. Shareholders vote on splits; they do not vote on stock dividends.

    Priority in liquidation

    If a company dissolves, its assets are sold and the cash is paid out in a strict order. Common stockholders are last in line and receive only what is left, which is often nothing.

    A fictional rocket company with $90M of claims ranked ahead of common stock. Drag the slider to set how much cash the liquidation raises and watch who gets paid.

    Exam tip · Priority in liquidation

    Unpaid wages → unpaid taxes → secured creditors → unsecured creditors (general creditors and debenture holders) → subordinated debt holders → preferred stockholders → common stockholders last. Stockholders can never be asked to cover the company's debts: limited liability.

    Transfer

    You can sell or give away your shares freely. The company's transfer agent keeps the official list of owners. When you sell, the transfer agent cancels the seller's shares, records the buyer on the register, and issues the shares in the buyer's name. Records are electronic (book entry), and regular stock trades settle T+1: trade date plus one business day. The transfer agent also handles dividend payments and proxy mailings.

    Section 1.3Trading

    Primary and secondary markets

    In the primary market, a company sells new shares, for example in an initial public offering (IPO), and receives the cash. In the secondary market, investors trade existing shares with each other, and the company receives nothing. Almost all daily trading is secondary.

    Listed stocks trade on an exchange such as the NYSE or Nasdaq. They have to meet listing standards, and the large number of buyers and sellers makes them highly liquid: you can usually sell quickly at a fair price. Other stocks trade over the counter (OTC) through a network of dealers. These are often smaller or newer companies with fewer traders, so selling a large position can push the price down.

    Exam tip

    Primary = issuer raises capital. Secondary = investor to investor. OTC stocks carry more liquidity (marketability) risk than listed stocks.

    Authorized, issued, outstanding, treasury

    The four counts
    • Authorized: the maximum number of shares the corporate charter allows. Raising it takes a shareholder vote to amend the charter.
    • Issued: shares that have been sold to investors at some point. Issued can never exceed authorized.
    • Treasury: issued shares the company bought back and holds. They have no voting rights and receive no dividends. The company can reissue or retire them.
    • Outstanding: shares in investors' hands. Outstanding = Issued − Treasury. EPS, voting, and dividends are all based on this number.

    Market capitalization is the market's value of the whole company: market cap = share price × outstanding shares. Treasury shares don't count.

    Exam tip · Buybacks

    When a company repurchases shares, outstanding shares fall while earnings stay the same, so earnings per share rises. Treasury shares are still issued shares; they just are not outstanding.

    Three kinds of value

    Par value

    An accounting number

    An arbitrary dollar amount set when the stock is issued, often $1 or a fraction of a cent. For common stock it has no relationship to market price and matters only for bookkeeping. (Par is important for preferred stock and bonds, where payments are figured from it.)

    Book value

    What the balance sheet says

    Net worth per share: tangible assets minus all liabilities and preferred stock, divided by common shares outstanding. It is a rough estimate of what each share would get in a liquidation.

    Market value

    What someone will pay

    The price the stock trades at, set by supply and demand. It reflects what investors expect the company to earn, and it can sit far above or below book value.

    American Depositary Receipts

    How an ADR is made
    • A U.S. bank buys ordinary shares of a foreign company, held by a custodian bank in the company's home country.
    • The bank issues ADRs (receipts) against those shares. Each ADR can stand for one share, several, or a fraction, set by the ADR ratio.
    • ADRs trade in the U.S. in U.S. dollars on exchanges or over the counter, and dividends are converted and paid in dollars.
    • Holders can exchange ADRs for the underlying foreign shares.
    What to remember
    • Currency risk remains even though the ADR is priced in dollars. If the foreign currency weakens against the dollar, the ADR's value and dividends fall.
    • Political and country risk of the company's home market.
    • Sponsored ADRs are set up with the foreign company's participation, and holders usually get voting rights. Unsponsored ADRs are created by a bank without the company's involvement and usually have no voting rights.
    • Foreign taxes may be withheld from dividends, and the depositary bank charges fees.
    Exam tip · The purpose

    ADRs make it easier for U.S. investors to own foreign companies: no foreign brokerage account, no currency exchange at purchase, and U.S. settlement. They do not remove currency risk.

    Key terms
    Primary market
    Where new shares are sold and the issuer receives the money.
    Secondary market
    Where investors trade existing shares among themselves.
    OTC
    Over the counter: trading through a dealer network instead of an exchange.
    Treasury stock
    Shares the company bought back; issued but not outstanding, no vote, no dividend.
    Par value

    Book value

    ADR

    Section 1.4Suitability

    Why own common stock? It offers growth: there is no ceiling on how high a stock can go, and over the long run stocks have tended to outpace inflation, which makes them an inflation hedge. It can also provide income, mostly from blue-chip companies that pay steady dividends. And listed shares are liquid: you can usually sell them quickly.

    The trade-off is volatility. Prices swing, sometimes sharply, and common stock sits last in line if the company fails.

    Two families of risk

    SystematicNon-systematic
    What it hitsThe whole marketOne company or one sector
    ExamplesMarket risk, inflation (purchasing power) risk, interest rate riskBusiness, financial, concentration, regulatory, legislative, political, liquidity
    DiversificationDoes not helpReduces it

    Holding a single stock exposes you to concentration risk: one bad contract award or failed launch can sink the whole position. Adding more, different stocks makes company-specific risk fade, but the market-wide floor never moves. That is why diversification reduces non-systematic risk only.

    Exam tip

    "Systematic" sounds like "system": the whole system goes down together.

    Common stock suits investors with a long time horizon who can tolerate price swings. It is not the place for money needed soon.

    Section 1.5Fundamental analysis

    Fundamental analysis judges a company by its financial statements. The SIE focuses on two: the balance sheet and the income statement.

    The balance sheet

    The balance sheet is a snapshot at a single point in time of what a company owns (assets) and owes (liabilities). What's left over belongs to the owners:

    Assets − Liabilities = Net worth (stockholders' equity)

    CategoryExamples
    Current assetsCash, accounts receivable, inventory, prepaid expenses
    Fixed assetsReal estate and land, equipment, vehicles, furniture
    Intangible assetsPatents, trademarks, copyrights, trade secrets
    Current liabilitiesAccounts payable; wages, taxes and interest payable; loan principal due within 12 months
    Long-term liabilitiesBonds and notes due in over a year, mortgages, pensions
    Exam tip

    "Current" means within one year. Receivable = owed to you. Payable = you owe it.

    Stockholders' equity has three parts. Suppose a company sells 1,000,000 shares at $20 with a $1 par value:

    • Par value: 1,000,000 × $1 = $1M
    • Capital in excess of par (paid-in surplus): 1,000,000 × $19 = $19M
    • Retained earnings: profits the company kept instead of paying out as dividends

    The income statement

    The income statement covers a period of time, such as a quarter or a year. It starts with sales and subtracts costs step by step. Using a fictional company with numbers in $ millions:

    LineCalculation$M
    RevenueSales1,000
    Gross profitRevenue − cost of goods sold (600)400
    EBIT (operating income)Gross profit − operating expenses (200)200
    EBTEBIT − interest (50)150
    Net incomeEBT − taxes (21% = 31.5)118.5
    Retained earnings (added)Net income − dividends (40)78.5
    Exam tip

    EBIT = operating income. Net income = profit after everything. Net income − dividends = the addition to retained earnings, which flows to the balance sheet.

    Earnings per share and the P/E ratio

    Earnings per share (EPS) = net income ÷ shares outstanding. The price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio = share price ÷ EPS. A stock at $80 with EPS of $4 has a P/E of 20: investors pay $20 for every $1 of annual earnings.

    A high P/E usually signals that investors expect strong growth; a low P/E can point to a value stock, or to a company the market is worried about.

    Key terms
    Balance sheet
    A snapshot of assets, liabilities and net worth at one point in time.
    Income statement
    Revenue, costs and profit over a period of time.
    Retained earnings
    Profits kept in the business instead of paid as dividends.
    EPS
    Net income ÷ shares outstanding.
    P/E ratio
    Share price ÷ EPS.
