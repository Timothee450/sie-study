Two ways to make money

① Capital appreciation Sell for more than you paid → +$15 a share
② Cash dividends The company shares its profits in cash

Where do the profits go?

Growth company · e.g. Amazon
Profits↻Back into the business

New products, expansion, R&D. Little or no dividend. You're betting on the price rising.

Mature company · e.g. Ford
Excess profits→Paid to shareholders

Fewer big growth projects, so extra cash goes out as dividends.

Exam tipMost common stocks pay no dividend at all. Growth = reinvest; mature = pay out.

Cyclical swings. Defensive holds.

Flip the economy

Exam tipCyclical = things people can skip (cars, restaurants, hotels). Defensive = things people always need (utilities, healthcare, food).

Exam tip"Pro-rata" = in proportion to the shares you own.

Product

Four dates, in DERP order

Declaration→ Ex-date→ Record→ Payable
  • Board sets Declaration, Record and Payable
  • The exchange sets the ex-date

Exam tipThe price drops by about the dividend on the ex-date.

Statutory vs cumulative voting

Win a seat

3 board seats open. A big holder (200 shares) backs A, B, C with 200 votes each. You own 100 shares and back Y.

Rule for both1 share = 1 vote per seat

100 shares × 3 seats = 300 votes

StatutoryMax 100 votes on any one seat
CumulativeStack all 300 on one candidate

Exam tipCumulative voting helps smaller shareholders. Statutory favors the big holders.

Preemptive rights stop dilution

Try it

You own 50,000 of 500,000 shares (10%). The company sells new shares.

Your shares
50,000
Your stake
5%
  • New shares shrink your % (dilution)
  • Preemptive right: buy new shares first, before the public
  • Buy your share of the new issue → keep your %

Exam tipWithout offering rights, dilutive moves (new shares, convertibles, warrants, employee options) need shareholder approval.

A right is born in the money

  • 1 right for each share you own
  • Short life: about 30–45 days
  • Your choice: exercise, sell it, or let it expire

Exam tipRights are priced below market, so they have value the day they're issued.

1.3

Trading

Primary vs secondaryExchange vs OTCAuthorized → outstandingMarket cap

Who gets your money?

Primary market · IPO / new issue
Investor$ →Issuer

The company sells new shares and receives the cash.

Secondary market · everyday trading
Investor$ ⇄Investor

Owners trade with each other. The company gets nothing.

Exam tipPrimary = issuer raises capital. Secondary = investor to investor.

Where stocks trade

Exchange-listed · NYSE, Nasdaq
Meets listing standards. Lots of buyers and sellers.

High liquidity: easy to sell fast at a fair price.

Over-the-counter (OTC)
Dealer network. Often smaller, newer companies.

Fewer traders, so selling can push the price down.

Exam tipOTC stocks carry more liquidity (marketability) risk than listed stocks.

  • Authorized: max the charter allows
  • Issued: ever sold to investors

Exam tipTreasury shares get no votes and no dividends. Market cap = price × outstanding shares.

Three kinds of value

ParAn accounting number, e.g. $1

Nothing to do with price.

BookAssets − liabilities − preferred, per share

A rough liquidation value.

MarketWhat buyers pay today

Set by supply & demand.

Exam tipOnly market value tells you what your shares are worth right now.

ADRs: foreign stock, U.S. dollars

Foreign shares→ U.S. bank (custodian abroad)→ ADRs trade in the U.S. in $
  • Dividends paid in dollars
  • Still carries currency risk
  • Sponsored ADRs usually vote; unsponsored usually don't

Exam tipADRs make foreign stocks easy to buy. They do not remove currency risk.

Balance sheet: a snapshot

  • Shows what a company owns and owes
  • At a single point in time
Assets − Liabilities = Net worth
(also called stockholders' equity)

What goes where

Current assets
  • Cash
  • Accounts receivable
  • Inventory
  • Prepaid expenses
Fixed assets
  • Real estate, land
  • Equipment
  • Vehicles
  • Furniture
Intangible assets
  • Patents
  • Trademarks
  • Copyrights
  • Trade secrets
Current liabilities
  • Accounts payable
  • Wages, taxes, interest payable
  • Loan principal due within 12 months
Long-term liabilities
  • Bonds & notes over 1 year
  • Mortgages
  • Pensions

Exam tip"Current" means within one year. Receivable = owed to you. Payable = you owe it.

Income statement: a period of time

Build the statement$ millions
Revenue − COGS = Gross profit − OpEx = EBIT − Interest = EBT − Taxes = Net income − Dividends = Retained earnings

Exam tipEBIT = operating income. Net income = profit after everything. Retained earnings flow to the balance sheet.
